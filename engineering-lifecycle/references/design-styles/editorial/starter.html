Water

The river that runs backwards

For six weeks each summer the Murray reverses through the Barmah choke. A century of engineering assumed it never would.

Words by Rosalind Whitcombe  |  Photographs by Aiden Kirkhope  |  14 minute read

The choke is nine kilometres of narrow channel where the Murray squeezes between two blocks of red gum forest. Upstream it carries 9,300 megalitres a day. Through the choke it manages 8,500. The difference has to go somewhere, and for most of the twentieth century the answer was that it went onto the floodplain, which was treated as a loss to be engineered away.

Kelvin Ngarrindjeri has been measuring flow at Picnic Point since 1987. His records are the longest continuous dataset anyone has for the reach, and they are kept in forty-one exercise books in a shed behind his house.

What the exercise books show

Between late December and early February, in eleven of the last thirty-eight years, the gauge at Picnic Point reads negative for periods of two to nine days. Water is moving upstream. The volumes are small, rarely more than 40 megalitres a day, but the direction is the point.

The Murray Darling Basin Authority's hydrological model does not represent this. It cannot: the model resolves the choke as a single reach with a single direction of travel, which was a reasonable simplification in 1988 and has been carried forward through four major revisions since.

Forty-one exercise books, and not one of them has ever been cited in a basin plan. Kelvin Ngarrindjeri, Picnic Point
